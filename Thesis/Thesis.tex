    The realisation of commercial nuclear fusion via the deuterium-Tritium (D-T) fuel cycle is
    contingent upon achieving tritium self-sufficiency. This project utilises a coupled
    computational workflow, integrating Paramak for parametric CAD generation and OpenMC for
    stochastic neutron transport, to optimise the neutronic performance of tokamak breeder blankets.
    A systematic parameter sweep was conducted to evaluate the impact of material composition,
    isotopic enrichment, and reactor geometry on the Tritium Breeding Ratio (TBR).

    Results indicate that the liquid eutectic \ce{Li17Pb83} offers superior performance, achieving a
    maximum TBR of 1.61, significantly outperforming molten salts. Solid ceramic concepts provide
    more consistent strong TRB performace of $1.48 \pm 0.03$. Contrary to high-enrichment

    homogenous approximation of the breeder blanket structure,  water coolant was found to enhance
    breeding via moderation, gaseous coolants (\ce{He}, \ce{N2}) act as voids that degrade the
    neutron economy.

    While these findings suggest several avenues to optimise TBR, informing design constraints for
    next-generation devices, focusing on TBR optimisation can lead to compromises in other key
    parameters required for realising fusion like heat extraction. Further work in relaxing the
    model approximations will provide greater fidelity and applicability for these results.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The global demand for energy is rising inexorably, coinciding with an urgent necessity to
decarbonise power generation. In this context, nuclear fusion offers the prospect of a
near-limitless, low-carbon energy source. By replicating the stellar nucleosynthesis processes that
power the Sun, fusion energy aims to generate electricity without long-lived high-activity
radioactive waste or the risk of runaway chain reactions \cite{nuclearphysics}.

Among the various reaction pathways, the deuterium-tritium (D-T) fuel cycle is the most viable for
near-term applications due to its high reaction cross-section at achievable plasma temperatures
\cite{heckrotte_factors_1971}. However, while deuterium is abundant in earth's oceans, the practical
realisation of this cycle faces a fundamental logistical challenge: the supply of tritium. Tritium
is a radioactive isotope ($\tau_{1/2} \approx 12.3$ years) with no significant natural abundance
\cite{brown_endfb-viii0_2018}. Current global inventories are derived as by-products of heavy water
moderated fission reactors and are insufficient to support a future fleet of commercial fusion power
plants (see Section~\ref{sec:tritium_literature}).

Consequently, a D-T fusion reactor cannot rely on an external fuel supply; it must breed its own
fuel. This leads to the concept of the \textit{Breeder Blanket}—a component designed to intercept
the 14.1 MeV neutrons produced in the fusion reaction and transmute lithium into tritium. While the
blanket serves integral roles in heat extraction and shielding, its defining function—and the
primary figure of merit for this study—is the \textbf{Tritium Breeding Ratio (TBR)}: the ratio of
tritium produced to tritium consumed.

Because the D-T reaction produces only one neutron per consumption event, this ``one-to-one" neutron
economy would require breeding efficiency (no losses of neutrons / tritium). Real world breeding
inefficiencies (see Section~\ref{sec:blanket_literature}) necessitate achieving a TBR greater than
1.0. As the source of fuel for fusion, tritium breeding is not merely a design objective but a
threshold requirement for the viability and scalability of fusion energy \cite{sawan_physics_2006}.

This strict margin necessitates high-fidelity modelling. Experimental validation of full-scale
reactor neutronics is generally unfeasible, placing the burden of design on computational
simulation. This project applies advanced Monte Carlo transport methods to perform a systematic
optimisation of the breeder blanket. By utilising a parametric computational workflow, this study
aims to identify the specific material compositions and geometric configurations required to
maximise the TBR, ensuring fuel self-sufficiency while maintaining adequate performance for other
aspects of fusion energy generation. The outcomes of this work are intended to inform the design of
next-generation concepts relevant to future magnetic confinement devices, such as the EU-DEMO
(DEMOnstration power plant) \cite{federici_european_2017} and the UK STEP (Spherical Tokamak for
Energy Production) programme \cite{lord_fusing_2024}.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Fundamentals of Nuclear Fusion}
\label{sec:fundamentals_theory}
Nuclear fusion is the process by which two light atomic nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus,
releasing energy due to the increase in total nuclear binding energy \cite{nuclearphysics}. While
various light nuclei interactions can release energy via this mass defect, the deuterium-tritium
(D-T) reaction has emerged as the most viable for near-term applications, partially due to
historical precedent (see Section~\ref{sec:tritium_literature}) but mainly due to its physical
accessibility.

This reaction is favoured because it possesses a significantly higher fusion cross-section
($\sigma_f$) at achievable plasma temperatures (10--20 keV) compared to alternatives such as D-D or
D-\ce{^3He} (see Figure \ref{fig:cross_sections}) \cite{heckrotte_factors_1971}. The reaction
proceeds as:

\begin{equation}
    \ce{^2H + ^3H -> n + \upalpha} + \ce{17.6 MeV}
    \label{eq:DT_reaction}
\end{equation}

The release of 17.6 MeV is partitioned inversely to the mass of the products to conserve momentum.
Consequently, the electrically neutral neutron carries the majority of the energy ($E_n = 14.1$
MeV), while the charged alpha particle ($\upalpha$) retains 3.5 MeV \cite{heckrotte_factors_1971}.

In Magnetic Confinement Fusion (MCF) devices, such as the tokamak \cite{wesson}, the charged
$\upalpha$-particle is confined by the magnetic field, depositing its energy to sustain the plasma
temperature (self-heating). The 14.1 MeV neutron, being electrically neutral, escapes the magnetic
confinement. The reactor breeder blanket is the subsystem designed to intercept this
neutron flux to extract thermal energy, shield the external components, and breed fuel
\cite{shatalov_breeder_1991}.

    \caption{A comparison of fusion reaction reactivities. The total cross-section is plotted as a
    function of ion temperature (keV) for the D-T, D-D, and D-\ce{^3He} fuel cycles. The D-T
    reaction peaks at a lower temperature and higher cross-section than alternatives
    \cite{heckrotte_factors_1971}.}
    \label{fig:cross_sections}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Tritium Breeding and Neutron Multiplication}
The necessity of tritium production was established in Section~\ref{sec:intro}. To achieve this, the
blanket must breed tritium via neutron capture interactions with lithium. Natural lithium consists
of two isotopes: \ce{^6Li} and \ce{^7Li} (1.9-7.8\% \ce{^6Li} abundance)
\cite{brown_endfb-viii0_2018}.

\subsubsection{Breeding Reactions}
Both isotopes undergo tritium-producing reactions, but their neutronic characteristics differ
fundamentally:

\begin{equation}
    \ce{^6Li + n -> \upalpha} + \ce{t + 4.8 MeV}
    \label{eq:Li6_reaction}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \ce{^7Li + n -> \upalpha} + \ce{t + n^\prime{}} - \ce{2.5 MeV}
    \label{eq:Li7_reaction}
\end{equation}

The \ce{^6Li} reaction (Eq. \ref{eq:Li6_reaction}) is exothermic and possesses a $1/v$ cross-section
($\sigma$) dependence, making it especially effective at thermal neutron energies ($E < 0.5$ eV)
(Figure \ref{fig:lithium_xs}) \cite{hernandez_first_2018}. Conversely, the \ce{^7Li} reaction (Eq.
\ref{eq:Li7_reaction}) is endothermic with a threshold energy of approximately 2.5 MeV
\cite{shatalov_breeder_1991}, and has a cross section orders of magnitude lower. But in
equation~\ref{eq:Li7_reaction} \ce{^7Li} releases a lower energy neutron, so does not deplete
neutron flux like \ce{^6Li}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/lithium_cs.png}
    \caption{Neutron cross-sections for the primary tritium breeding reactions,
    \ce{^6Li}(n,$\upalpha$)t and \ce{^7Li}(n, n$^\prime{}\upalpha$)t, as a function of incident
    neutron energy. Note the dominance of \ce{^6Li} at thermal energies. Data aquired from ENDF
    \cite{brown_endfb-viii0_2018}}
    \label{fig:lithium_xs}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Neutron Multiplication and Economy}
\label{sec:multiplication_theory}
The reactor's fuel self-sufficiency is quantified by the \textbf{Tritium Breeding Ratio (TBR)},
defined as the ratio of tritium bred to tritium consumed.

However, the D-T reaction produces exactly one neutron, and the primary \ce{^6Li} breeding reaction
consumes one neutron. This 1-to-1 economy leaves no margin for breeding inefficiency (see
Section~\ref{sec:blanket_literature}). To overcome this deficit, the blanket must utilise neutron
multipliers---materials with significant ``spallation" \ce{(n, 2n)} cross-sections at 14.1 MeV.
The primary candidates are beryllium and lead (see
Section~\ref{sec:neutron_multiplication_literature}):

\begin{equation}
    \ce{^9Be + n -> ^8Be} + \ce{2n^\prime{} - 1.57MeV}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \ce{^{208}Pb + n -> ^{207}Pb + 2n^\prime{} - 7.37MeV}
\end{equation}

These are threshold reactions (see Figure \ref{fig:multiplier_xs}) that increase the macroscopic
neutron population, allowing a single incident fusion neutron to potentially breed more than one
triton \cite{hernandez_first_2018}. Beryllium has a much lower cross section, but is able to
spallate at lower neutron energyies ($\sim$2.5MeV). Though lead requires higher neutron energies
($\sim$8MeV), it has a significantly higher cross section.

    \label{fig:multiplier_xs}

\subsection{Neutron Transport and Interactions}
To determine the TBR, one must model the full phase-space behaviour of neutrons within the complex
reactor geometry. This behaviour is governed by the Boltzmann Transport Equation (BTE).

\subsubsection{The Boltzmann Transport Equation}
\label{sec:BTE}

The BTE is a linear integro-differential equation describing the conservation of neutrons in phase
space $(\vec{r}, \hat{\Omega}, E, t)$. It stipulates that the rate of change of neutron density is
equal to the net production minus net loss and net leakage. For a steady-state system with a fixed
source, the BTE is given by \cite{kuridan_neutron_2023}:

\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{\hat{\Omega} \cdot \nabla \psi}_{\text{Streaming}} + \underbrace{\Sigma_t \psi}_{\text{Interaction Loss}} = \underbrace{\int_0^\infty \int_{4\pi} \Sigma_s(E^\prime \to E, \hat{\Omega}^\prime \to \hat{\Omega}) \psi^\prime d\hat{\Omega}^\prime dE^\prime}_{\text{Backscattering Source}} + \underbrace{S(\vec{r}, \hat{\Omega}, E)}_{\text{Fixed Source}}
    \label{eq:BTE}
\end{equation}

Where $\psi(\vec{r}, \hat{\Omega}, E)$ is the angular neutron flux, $\Sigma_t$ is the total
macroscopic cross-section, and $\Sigma_s$ represents the probability of a neutron scattering from
energy $E^\prime$ and direction $\hat{\Omega}^\prime$ into $E$ and $\hat{\Omega}$.

Analytic solutions to Eq. \ref{eq:BTE} are generally impossible for heterogeneous 3D reactor
geometries. Consequently, this project employs Monte Carlo (MC) methods. MC does not solve the
equation for average flux directly; instead, it simulates the stochastic histories of individual
particles, sampling interaction probabilities from nuclear data libraries such as ENDF/B-VIII.0
\cite{brown_endfb-viii0_2018}. The statistical aggregation of these histories converges to the
solution of the integral BTE \cite{kuridan_neutron_2023} (see Section~\ref{sec:MC}).

\subsubsection{Interaction Probabilities, Mean Free Path and Moderation}
\label{sec:meanfreepath_theory}
The probability of a specific interaction type $x$ (e.g., capture, fission, scatter) occurring is
defined by the macroscopic cross-section $\Sigma_x$, which relates to the microscopic cross-section
$\sigma_x$ and atomic density $N$:
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_x(E) = N \sigma_x(E)
    \label{eq:macroscopic_cross_section}
\end{equation}
The mean free path ($\lambda$), representing the average distance a neutron travels through the
blanket medium before interacting \cite{nuclearphysics}, is:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(E) = \frac{1}{\Sigma_{total}(E)}
    \label{eq:mean_free_path}
\end{equation}
In blanket design, $\lambda$ dictates the necessary radial build thickness required to attenuate the
14.1 MeV flux and protect against neutron leakage \cite{tas_liquid_1988}.

Fast fusion neutrons can be moderated, often all the way to thermal energies, using appropriate low
mass number materials (such as water). This is detailed further in Appendix
\ref{app:neutronics_theory}. This maximises the cross section of the breeding reaction
\ref{eq:Li6_reaction}, and can reduce neutron leakage by attenuating neutron flux to thermal
energies.



\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit_review}

\subsection{The Tritium Fuel Imperative}
\label{sec:tritium_literature}

Given the physical accessibility of DT reactions (see Section~\ref{sec:fundamentals_theory}), they
have been the front runner for fusion since the post war boom of nuclear research; attempting to
replicate the success of fission \cite{paris_lost_2023}. As referenced in Section~\ref{sec:intro},
tritium supply is critical constraint on the realisation of DT fusion. Current global tritium
stockpiles are derived almost exclusively as a by-product of heavy-water moderated fission reactors
(CANDU, CANada DeUterium Recators) \cite{kovari_tritium_2018}. Analyses of this supply chain
indicate that stockpiles are insufficient to support the operation of a future fusion fleet
\cite{kovari_tritium_2018, pearson_tritium_2018}. The testing and startup of a large scale fusion
device like ITER alone is currently predicted to require $\sim$12.3kg, and the total production of
tritium per is (in optimistic scenarios) capped at 8kg per year. This ignores the consumption
estimates of operational fusion reactors which range from 10-100kg of tritium per year
\cite{lord_fusing_2024}. Consequently, the external supply chain is considered a bottleneck that
threatens the viability of the entire D-T fusion concept. To resolve this, commercial reactors are
required to breed their own fuel in-situ via the \textbf{Tritium Breeder Blanket}.

\subsection{Breeder Blanket Evolution and Functions}
\label{sec:blanket_literature}

The concept of a ``breeder'' blanket predates magnetic confinement fusion; it was originally
conceived to utilise high-energy neutrons to produce fissile materials (\ce{^{233}U}, \ce{^{239}Pu})
for fission warheads and reactors \cite{moir_fusion_1982, lee_us-doe_1983, maniscalco_fusion_1984}.
Modern fusion research has repurposed this architecture to focus on the transmutation of lithium
into tritium. A ``blanket" refers to a structure that lines the walls of a fusion reactor;
``breeder'' indicates that tritium is produced upon interacting with neutrons. Beyond breeding, the
blanket must simultaneously perform three conflicting functions: shielding superconducting magnets
and external components from neutron damage / radiation, extracting high-grade heat for power
conversion, and maintaining a TBR $>$ 1.0 \cite{shatalov_breeder_1991}.
Fusion projects across the world are already outlining blanket designs as a key strategic focus,
some examples include ITER's DEMO \cite{federici_overview_2019}, UK's STEP \cite{lord_fusing_2024}
and the Chinese Fusion Engineering Test Reactor Facility (CFETR) \cite{liu_design_2022}.

TBR must be $>1.0$ give margin for several different sources of ineffeciency. Neutrons can be lost
to structural materials (e.g. first walls) via parasitic absorption and neutron can also leak out of
the reactor vessel. Produced tritium can be lost from processing inefficiencies and tritium's short
half life leads to decay losses. Accounting for these breeding inefficiencies literature typically
suggests a TBR significantly $> 1.0$ (typically $\approx 1.1$) \cite{shatalov_breeder_1991,
sawan_physics_2006, meschini_modeling_2023, federici_overview_2019}.

\subsection{Neutron Multiplication Strategies}
\label{sec:neutron_multiplication_literature}

Since natural lithium cannot sustain a TBR $>$ 1 in a realistic reactor geometry solely via the
endothermic $\ce{^{7}Li(n, n^\prime\upalpha)t}$ reaction, design studies have converged on two
primary multiplier candidates due to their high spallation cross section: beryllium and lead
\cite{hernandez_first_2018}.

Beryllium (\ce{^{9}Be}) can be considered superior due to its low threshold energy for the \ce{(n,
2n)} reaction. It is the multiplier of choice for solid ceramic breeder concepts (e.g., HCPB, WCPB)
and molten fluoride salts (FLiBe) \cite{wang_current_2019, tas_liquid_1988, sorbom_arc_2015}.
However, its application is controversial due to resource scarcity and high toxicity
\cite{shimwell_reducing_2016}. Shimwell et al. This highlights the handling of toxic beryllium dust
poses severe safety and manufacturing challenges, prompting research into minimizing its volume
fraction in blanket designs.

Conversely, lead (\ce{Pb}) is favored for higher \ce{(n, 2n)} cross section, where its threshold
energy is typically met for DT fusion ($E_n \sim 14.1$). It also has chemical compatibility
with liquid breeders specifically in the lithium-lead eutectic (\ce{Li17Pb83}). This alloy allows
the medium to function simultaneously as breeder, multiplier, and coolant \cite{wu_overview_2011}.
While lead mitigates the toxicity concerns of beryllium, it introduces neutronic penalties; it acts
as a high-Z shield, parasitically absorbing the thermal neutrons required for efficient \ce{^{6}Li}
breeding. Furthermore, the activation of lead can generate long-lived radioisotopes, complicating
decommissioning \cite{bocci_arc_2020}. The selection of a multiplier therefore represents a
trade-off between the superior neutronic efficiency of beryllium and the integrated engineering
advantages of lead \cite{hernandez_first_2018}.

\subsection{Divergence in Blanket Architectures}
\label{sec:blanket_architectures_literature}
Blanket designs have historically diverged into two competing pathways—liquid and solid—driven
primarily by the phase of the breeding medium and secondarily by the choice of coolant (water vs.
helium).

\subsubsection{Liquid Breeder Concepts}
Liquid breeders offer the distinct advantage of continuous tritium extraction and simplified fuel
replenishment \cite{tas_liquid_1988}. The most common liquid breeder is the eutectic alloy
lithium-lead (\ce{Li-Pb}). These designs generally require a dedicated coolant loop to manage
thermal loads:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{WCLL and HCLL:} The water-cooled lithium-lead (WCLL) \cite{federici_overview_2019}
    and helium-cooled lithium-lead (HCLL) \cite{wu_overview_2011, chen_conceptual_2015} concepts are
    primary candidates for ITER and also investigated for the CFETR. In these designs, the liquid
    metal serves primarily as a breeder and neutron multiplier, while heat extraction is handled by
    the separate coolant system. Some nitrogen coolant variants also exist
    \cite{fradera_pre-conceptual_2021}.
    \item \textbf{DCLL:} A more advanced variant, the dual-cooled lithium-lead (DCLL) blanket, is a
    mature concept for the European DEMO \cite{fernandez-berceruelo_progress_2024}. Here, the
    flowing liquid metal contributes significantly to cooling alongside a helium loop, increasing
    overall thermal efficiency \cite{graham_dual_2022}.
\end{itemize}

However, the flow of liquid metal through the tokamak's strong magnetic field induces significant
magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) drag, which can inhibit circulation
\cite{malang_self-cooled_1991,morley_thermofluid_2005}.

\paragraph{Molten Salts:} To mitigate these MHD issues, designs such as the ARC reactor utilise
molten salts like FLiBe \ce{(2LiF . BeF2)} which possess low electrical conductivity
\cite{sorbom_arc_2015, segantin_optimisation_2020}. While this solves the magnetic drag issue, these
salts introduce new challenges regarding chemical corrosion and complex flow chemistry control
\cite{bocci_arc_2020, bohm_initial_2023}.

\subsubsection{Solid Breeder Concepts}
Solid breeders utilise lithium ceramics (e.g., \ce{Li2TiO3}, \ce{Li4SiO4}) in packed pebble beds
combined with distinct coolant architectures. This approach eliminates MHD effects entirely, allows
for easier tritium extraction and ensures chemical stability \cite{proust_solid_1991,
knitter_recent_2013}.

The choice of coolant dictates the blanket structure and affects neutron transport efficiency:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HCPB:} Helium-cooled pebble bed, a primary focus of the DEMO system
    \cite{wang_current_2019,chaudhuri_overview_2013}.
    \item \textbf{WCPB:} Water-cooled pebble bed, the CFETR's prioritised approach
    \cite{lei_design_2020,liu_design_2022}.
\end{itemize}

While mechanically stable, the primary drawback of solid breeders lies in the complexity of tritium
extraction; tritium produced within the ceramic lattice must diffuse out and be purged by a sweep
gas, a process sensitive to radiation-induced trapping \cite{shi_density_2015}.

\subsection{Neutronics Modelling and the Research Gap}
\label{sec:research_gap}

Optimising these complex designs requires high-fidelity modelling, as experimental validation of
full-scale blanket neutronics is currently impossible \cite{delaporte-mathurin_advancing_2025}.
Consequently, the field relies on Monte Carlo (MC) transport codes (e.g., OpenMC, MCNP) to resolve
the stochastic behaviour of neutrons in 3D geometries \cite{romano_openmc_2015, bae_arc_2022}.

However, a critical review of the literature reveals a methodological gap in how these tools are
applied. Previous studies generally fall into one of two categories:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Broad Material Surveys:} Reviews such as \cite{hernandez_first_2018} provide
    excellent ``first principles'' comparisons of many materials but rely on simplified 1D or
    homogenised models. These studies sacrifice geometric accuracy for breadth, failing to capture
    spatial transport effects like streaming or leakage.
    \item \textbf{Specific Point-Designs:} Studies focused on specific reactors (e.g., ARC
    \cite{segantin_neutronic_2020} or STEP \cite{fradera_pre-conceptual_2021}) utilize high-fidelity
    3D CAD models but fix the geometry to a single design point. These studies answer ``What is the
    performance of \textit{this} reactor?'' rather than ``How does geometry influence performance?''
\end{enumerate}

This dichotomy exists because manually generating unique 3D CAD models for a parametric sweep has
historically been computationally intractable. Recent developments in parametric CAD generation have
begun to bridge this gap. Goel et al. \cite{goel_optimisation_2023} demonstrated the viability of
automated geometry generation for neutronic sweeps. However, their study—representing an initial
step in this domain—did not fully exploit the coupling between material composition and geometric
variance.

This project aims to close this gap by applying an automated, parametric workflow to a realistic 3D
tokamak model. Unlike previous studies which isolated material choice from geometric design, this
work will conduct a systematic parameter sweep of variables—including varying \ce{^{6}Li}
enrichment, blanket thickness, and material ratios. This approach allows for the identification of
optimal design points that lie at the intersection of material science and reactor geometry, regions
of the design space that effectively remain unmapped in current literature.



\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Modelling Neutron Transport: Monte Carlo}
\label{sec:MC}

The neutronic performance of the breeder blanket is determined by the transport behaviour of the
entire neutron population within the reactor geometry under the BTE (equation~\ref{eq:BTE}). As
explained in Section~\ref{sec:BTE}, the \textbf{Monte Carlo (MC) method} is used to solve for
neutron transport behaviour. The MC method is a stochastic technique that simulates the transport of
neutrons by tracking the individual life histories of discrete particles \cite{kalos_monte_2008}.
Rather than solving for global flux directly, the simulation proceeds through a random walk process:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Source Sampling:} A neutron is initialized at the plasma source with sampled
    parameters (e.g., 14.1~MeV energy and isotropic direction).
    \item \textbf{Ray Tracing:} The distance to the next interaction is sampled from a probability
    distribution derived from the total macroscopic cross-section of the local material
    \cite{brown_endfb-viii0_2018}.
    \item \textbf{Interaction Physics:} At the collision site, the specific reaction type (elastic
    scatter, absorption, \ce{(n, 2n)} multiplication, etc.) is selected probabilistically based on
    the nuclide-specific cross-section data.
    \item \textbf{History Termination:} The particle's energy and direction are updated after
    scattering. The process repeats until the neutron is absorbed or leaks from the system geometry.
    Multiplication events generate secondary neutrons which are subsequently tracked.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Statistical Uncertainty and Error Analysis}
Unlike deterministic solvers, Monte Carlo results represent statistical estimates rather than exact
solutions. The physical quantities of interest, such as the TBR, are
tallied by averaging contributions across many independent particle histories. The uncertainty in
these results is quantified using the standard deviation of the mean across simulation batches.

The relative error ($R$) in an MC calculation is governed by the Central Limit Theorem and scales
inversely with the square root of the number of simulated particles
\cite{kuridan_neutron_2023,romano_openmc_2015} ($N$):
\begin{equation}
    R \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
\end{equation}
To ensure statistical robustness, the simulations in this work utilised high particle counts
(typically $N \sim 10^5 - 10^7$) to drive convergence. This resulted in relative errors
significantly below 1\%.

\subsection{Computational Workflow and Toolchain}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:lit_review}, modern neutronics research relies on a robust
toolchain linking geometric modeling with particle transport simulation. This project employs a
coupled workflow utilising two primary open-source tools: \textbf{Paramak} for geometry
generation and \textbf{OpenMC} for neutron transport.

\subsubsection{Geometry Generation: Paramak}
To manage the complex toroidal geometry of the tokamak, this project utilises \textbf{Paramak}, a
Python-based parametric CAD framework \cite{shimwell_paramak_2021}. Paramak allows for the
automated generation of tokamak fusion reactor models through a high-level API, enabling the rapid
modification of geometric features—such as blanket thickness, triangularity, and radial
build—without manual CAD reconstruction.

The generated geometry is exported as a DAGMC (Direct Accelerated Geometry Monte Carlo) unstructured
mesh. This format is optimised for ray-tracing operations, allowing the Monte Carlo code to
transport particles directly on the CAD surface representation without voxelization.

\subsubsection{Neutron Transport: OpenMC}
All neutronic simulations are performed using \textbf{OpenMC}, a state-of-the-art Monte
Carlo transport code \cite{romano_openmc_2015}. OpenMC is widely adopted within the fusion
community due to its continuous-energy physics and efficient parallelisation. The code has been
extensively validated for complex fusion applications, demonstrating high fidelity in calculating
critical parameters such as the TBR and volumetric nuclear heating \cite{bae_arc_2022}.

To ensure computational efficiency while maintaining physical accuracy, the simulations utilise a
90° sectoral revolution of the tokamak with reflective periodic boundary conditions applied
to the radial surfaces (Figure~\ref{fig:tokamak_labels}).

\subsection{Definition of the Base Reactor Model}

Before undertaking parametric optimisation, a ``Base Model" was established to serve as a reference
point. The parameters for this baseline were synthesised from literature to ensure the reactor
possesses dimensions and material compositions representative of a realistic DEMO-class machine. A
summary of these base parameters is provided below.

\subsubsection{Neutron Source Specification}
The plasma source is modelled as an isotropic, 14.1 MeV neutron ring source located at the magnetic
axis. To approximate the Doppler broadening resulting from the thermal motion of plasma ions, the
energy spectrum follows a Muir distribution \cite{muir_sensitivity_1974}. The ion temperature is set
to 20 keV, consistent with burning plasma conditions described by Wandelt
\cite{wandelt_encyclopedia_2018}. Note that the plasma is treated solely as a neutron ring source; a
physical plasma volume with self and wall interactions are outside the scope of this neutronic
study.

\subsubsection{Breeder and Coolant Composition}
\label{sec:base_compositions}
The reference liquid breeder material is the eutectic lithium-lead alloy (\ce{Li17Pb83}). This
specific stoichiometry is selected for its low melting point, requiring less power to melt and
mitigating freezing concerns in the loop \cite{mas_de_les_valls_leadlithium_2008}. There is no hard
consensus regarding \ce{Li6} enrichment, literature varies from a low as 10\% all the way to 90\%
\cite{hernandez_first_2018,boullon_molten_2021,segantin_optimisation_2020,segantin_neutronic_2020,kurasawa_ceramic_1995}.
To take an average values of the most common values found in breeding studies, the \ce{^6Li}
enrichment is initialised at 60\%.

Pressurised liquid water (\ce{H_2O}) is selected as the base coolant due to its prevalence in
designs such as the ITER TBM and DEMO concepts \cite{hirose_functional_2024}, with helium and
nitrogen coolants considered (Section~\ref{sec:blanket_architectures_literature}). To maintain
computational tractability, the breeder and coolant are modelled as a homogenised mixture rather
than discrete cooling channels. The coolant occupies 33\% of the total blanket volume, as an
estimation of piping volume of typical setups \cite{wang_current_2019}.

For the comparative analysis of solid ceramic breeders, a specific multiplier configuration was
defined to ensure adequate neutron population, informed as a design common in a breadth of older and
more recent literature \cite{shatalov_breeder_1991,wang_current_2019,knitter_recent_2013}. These
simulations utilised a titanium-beryllide multiplier (\ce{Be12Ti}) mixed with the ceramic breeder at
a Breeder-to-Multiplier volume ratio of 1:3. To accurately approximate the macroscopic density of a
ceramic pebble-bed concept, a packing factor of 62\% was applied to the material definition,
accounting for the inter-particle void space inherent to packed beds.

\subsubsection{Reactor Geometry and First Wall}
\label{sec:base_geometry}

The first wall (FW) material is zirconium-alloy-4 (Zr-4: 98.2\% Zr, 1.5\% Sn, 0.2\% Fe, 0.1\% Cr).
Zirconium is selected over standard ferritic-martensitic steels for the base model due to its
significantly lower neutron absorption cross-section \cite{king_high_2022}. The FW thickness is set
to 25 mm, an average value derived from structural integrity studies \cite{boullon_molten_2021,
lei_design_2020}.

The breeder blanket depth is set to an average of 500 mm, consistent with modular designs found in
literature \cite{lei_design_2020, liu_design_2022}. The reactor triangularity is fixed at the preset
0.55 in the base model, which is a parameter that controls how triangular the cavity is for plasma
distortion which can improve plasma performance in real tokamaks \cite{wesson}.

\subsection{Simulation Strategy and Parametric Sweeps}

To explore the design space surrounding the Base Model, this project implements a custom, automated
simulation pipeline. Leveraging the Python interfaces of both Paramak and OpenMC,
an iterative script varies specific design parameters while holding the remaining Base Model
attributes constant.

The workflow for each parametric sweep follows this sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parameter Injection:} A target variable (e.g., Blanket Thickness, \ce{^6Li}
    Enrichment, or Coolant Fraction) is modified in the input dictionary.
    \item \textbf{Geometry Reconstruction:} Paramak regenerates the 3D CAD model and
    converts it to the DAGMC h5m format.
    \item \textbf{Material Assignment:} OpenMC applies the material definitions, updating
    isotopic fractions (e.g., adjusting the lead-lithium ratio) as required by the sweep.
    \item \textbf{Transport Simulation:} The stochastic transport simulation is executed, tallying
    neutron interactions in the blanket volume to determine the TBR.
\end{enumerate}

This methodology allows for the isolation of individual variable effects, providing clear trends on
how geometric and material changes influence the overall neutronic performance.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results presented in Section~\ref{sec:results} quantify the neutronic performance of various
breeder blanket concepts. While the simulation data identifies specific maxima for TBR, the
selection of a viable blanket design for a fusion power plant requires a holistic evaluation that
balances neutronic efficiency against thermal-hydraulic, structural, and economic constraints
\cite{hernandez_first_2018, federici_european_2017}.

\subsection{TBR Optimisation Strategies}

\subsubsection{Isotopic Optimisation}

A significant finding is the behaviour of \ce{^6Li} enrichment (Figure~\ref{fig:enrichment}). While
high enrichment is often assumed to be beneficial, our results indicate a saturation point at
approximately 40\% enrichment. Beyond this point, the blanket becomes ``neutron-limited'' rather
than ``target-limited.'' Furthermore, enrichment levels approaching 100\% actually degrade
performance. This is attributed to the total depletion of \ce{^7Li}, which removes the contribution
of the $\ce{^7Li(n, n^\prime{} \upalpha)t}$ reaction \cite{sawan_physics_2006}. Although this
reaction is less frequent, it preserves the neutron population by emitting a secondary neutron,
whereas the \ce{^6Li} reaction is purely absorptive. Economically, this suggests that costly
high-enrichment processes are unnecessary; a moderate enrichment of 20--40\% represents the optimal
trade-off between TBR and fuel cycle costs \cite{goel_optimisation_2023,
segantin_optimisation_2020}.

\subsubsection{Compositional Optimisation}

The study confirms that the eutectic lithium-lead alloy (\ce{Li17Pb83}) offered superior breeding
performance compared to molten salts, primarily driven by the neutron multiplication capabilities of
lead \cite{malang_self-cooled_1991}. However, the simulation highlights a critical physical
distinction: the superior performance of the eutectic alloy over non-eutectic mixtures
(Figure~\ref{fig:multiplier}) cannot be explained by stoichiometry alone. The eutectic formation
allows for a packing efficiency that results in a density approximately 15\% higher than the
weighted average of its constituents \cite{mas_de_les_valls_leadlithium_2008}. This increased
macroscopic cross-section is the dominant factor in maximising the neutron economy.

Molten salts generally resulted in lower TBRs compared to liquid metals but are investigated due to
their reduced Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) effects. Of the halide salts, fluorine-based compounds
(e.g., FLiBe) perform best due to their low neutron absorption cross-sections. Bromine salts exhibit
high parasitic absorption, leading to significantly higher heating values (approx. 15.5 MeV).
Despite this, bromine's moderating properties ensured it still retained higher TBR than chorine.

Among the other specific solid breeder compounds (Table~\ref{tab:solid_breeders}), cerium-based
ceramics achieved the highest TBR due to the low parasitic capture cross-sections of cerium.
Conversely, cobalt-based compounds act as outliers, yielding lower TBRs despite the high multiplier
fraction; this is attributed to the higher absorption and scattering cross-sections of cobalt, which
degrade the neutron economy. The average performance of the solid breeders was strong (TBR $= 1.48
\pm 0.03$) and consistent, higher TBR on average than liquid breeders. A large part of this
uniformity arises both from the relatively similar atomic compositions of the molecules (mostly
lithium and oxygen) but also the standardised multiplier configuration (\ce{Be12Ti} at a 1:3 ratio)
defined in Section~\ref{sec:base_compositions}. Nonetheless, noting the easier tritium extraction
and better heat performance in pebble beds \cite{knitter_recent_2013} could place solid breeders as
more suitable overall.

\subsubsection{Coolant Optimisation}

Water performed the best by a significant margin as a coolant. This is attributed to the moderating
properties of water, which thermalises the neutron spectrum. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:coolants},
the water coolant shifts the incident neutron population toward lower energies
(Figure~\ref{fig:lithium_xs}). Since the tritium production cross-section of $^6$Li is significantly
higher at thermal energies, this moderation compensates for the displacement of breeder volume at
lower coolant fractions. This moderation will also reduce the neutron leakage, and so improve
heating along with TBR. This then introduces the importance of considering neutron mean free path,
flux attenuation and neutron leakage within the blanket, leading us to geometric studies.

\subsubsection{Spatial and Geometric Optimisation}
The saturation of TBR at a radial depth of roughly 500 mm (Figure~\ref{fig:blanket_thicknesses}) can
be validated physically by considering the neutron mean free path
(equation~\ref{eq:mean_free_path}). Dominated by lead, the macroscopic total cross-section
(equation~\ref{eq:macroscopic_cross_section}) of the breeder mixture yields a mean free path of
$\lambda \approx 5.5$ cm for 14 MeV neutrons. A 500 mm blanket therefore represents approximately 9
mean free paths, sufficient to attenuate over 99.9\% of the incident flux
\cite{kuridan_neutron_2023}. Increasing the blanket thickness beyond this point provides no
neutronic benefit and would act only as dead weight, complicating remote maintenance and increasing
capital costs \cite{federici_overview_2019}.

\subsection{Consequences to Other Critical Criteria}

While this study maximised TBR, the ``optimal'' neutronic configurations often conflict with other
critical reactor criteria.

\subsubsection{Structural Integrity vs. Neutronics}
The use of zirconium-4 (Zr-4) as the first wall material yielded the highest TBR due to its low
parasitic absorption cross-section (Figure~\ref{fig:first_wall}) \cite{king_high_2022}. However,
Zr-4 is generally unsuitable for the extreme neutron fluence and thermal stress of a DEMO-class
reactor first wall \cite{king_high_2022}. This also highlights that, although Figure~\ref{fig:first_wall}
suggests setting the first wall as thin as possible, as heat constraints must be considered to limit
how thin it can be \cite{stornelli_ultra-fine_2024}. Standard Reduced Activation
Ferritic-Martensitic (RAFM) steels, such as EUROFER 97, are the engineering standard despite their
higher neutron penalty \cite{stornelli_ultra-fine_2024}. Our results suggest that transitioning from
an idealised Zr-4 wall to a realistic steel wall results in a TBR penalty of approximately 0.1 to
0.15.

\subsubsection{Safety and Coolant Selection}
The choice of coolant represents a stark trade-off between performance and safety. Water (\ce{H2O})
was shown to enhance TBR (Figure~\ref{fig:coolants}) significantly better than gaseous coolants due
to its moderating power, which softens the neutron spectrum into the thermal region where the
\ce{^6Li} cross-section is largest (Figure~\ref{fig:lithium_xs}). However, the use of water in a
lithium-based blanket introduces the risk of violent exothermic chemical reactions in the event of a
leak \cite{kawamura_overview_2024, hirose_functional_2024}. While helium avoids this chemical
reactivity, our results show it acts effectively as a void, increasing neutron leakage and degrading
TBR \cite{wang_current_2019}. A realistic design must therefore weigh the significant neutronic
advantage of water moderation against the engineering complexity of double-walled piping and
pressure suppression systems required to mitigate chemical hazards \cite{liu_design_2022}.

\subsubsection{Energy Extraction (Heating)}
A counter-intuitive trade-off exists regarding nuclear heating. While \ce{Li17Pb83} provided the
highest TBR, it exhibited lower average heating per neutron compared to ceramic breeders (Table
\ref{tab:liquid_breeders} vs Table \ref{tab:solid_breeders}). This is intrinsic to the physics of
the multiplier: the \ce{(n, 2n)} reaction in lead is endothermic, consuming incident energy to
liberate neutrons \cite{graham_dual_2022}. However \ce{Li17Pb83} has another draw: being eutectic
also reduces the melting point (100°C lower), which draws significantly less energy in operation
\cite{mas_de_les_valls_leadlithium_2008}. In contrast, the parasitic absorption in molten salts
yields high heating but low breeding \cite{bohm_initial_2023}. Solid breeders provide the best
heating, and decent TBR. For a commercial power plant, where electricity generation is as vital as
fuel self-sufficiency, the lower energy multiplication factor of lead-lithium systems is a distinct
disadvantage that must be factored into the overall plant efficiency calculations
\cite{meschini_modeling_2023}.

\subsection{Model Limitations}

The primary limitation of this study lies in the homogenisation of the blanket materials. By
treating the breeder and coolant as a uniform mixture, the simulation neglects ``streaming
effects'', where neutrons escape through the void spaces of coolant channels without interaction
\cite{fradera_pre-conceptual_2021}. This likely results in a slight overestimation of the TBR in our
results compared to a heterogeneous model.

Furthermore, the negligible impact of triangularity on TBR (Figure~\ref{fig:triangularity}) is
likely an artifact of the simplified neutron source definition. The model utilised a uniform ring
source which does not geometrically conform to the plasma shape variations associated with high
triangularity \cite{shimwell_paramak_2021}. In a physical tokamak, increasing triangularity
compresses the plasma volume and alters the view factor of the neutrons incident on the first wall,
an effect not captured by the current source definition \cite{creely_overview_2020}.

\subsection{Next Steps}

Future work will focus on increasing the fidelity of the simulation model to address the limitations
identified above.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heterogeneous Blanket Geometry:} Utilising the advanced features of Paramak to
    model discrete cooling channels and pebble beds to quantify the effects of neutron streaming
    \cite{shimwell_paramak_2021}. Additionally, accouting for packing factors for
    \ce{Li_{x}Pb_{100-x}} alloys could provide a more informed sweep of neutron multiplier ratios.
    \item \textbf{Plasma Source Definition:} Replacing the ring source with a parametric plasma
    source that accurately maps the spatial distribution of fusion events, allowing for a true
    investigation into the effects of plasma geometry \cite{fable_plasma_2018}.
    \item \textbf{Structural Integration:} Moving beyond the simplified structure of the few most
    relevant neutronics layers, and instead including a more complex structure including key
    components like the external toroidal field coils. This will provide a more conservative and
    realistic estimate of the global TBR \cite{federici_overview_2019, lei_design_2020}.

\end{itemize}

Beyond increasing model fidelity, future studies should adopt a more holistic design approach that
optimises other critical criteria alongside TBR, such as nuclear heating or safety considerations.
Coupled parameter sweep studies offer a robust framework for this multi-objective optimisation. A
particular focus should be placed on minimising leakage fractions; ensuring leakage
approaches zero is essential for maximising the neutron economy available for breeding and heating,
while simultaneously mitigating safety risks associated with external neutron radiation. Tracking
this parameter is therefore recommended to ensure global design optimality.



\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This project utilised a parametric Paramak-OpenMC workflow to optimise tokamak breeder blankets for
tritium self-sufficiency. By quantifying the impact of material composition and geometry, this study
identified the critical design constraints required to realise a TBR greater than unity for
practical devices, when accounting for additional breeding inefficiency in practise.

The eutectic alloy \ce{Li17Pb83} emerged as the optimal breeder, achieving a maximum TBR of 1.61.
This superior performance results from the high packing density of the eutectic phase and effective
\ce{(n, 2n)} multiplication in lead, significantly outperforming molten salt concepts while also
boasting lower melting points. Solid breeders are still a strong practical alternative due to better
heat retention, no MHD effects, easier tritium extraction and a strong TBR of $1.48 \pm 0.03$ under
our base reactor setup.

This study overturns the assumption that maximal enrichment is required; results indicate
TBR saturation at 40\% \ce{^6Li}. Enrichment beyond this threshold degrades performance by
eliminating the neutron-preserving \ce{^7Li(n,n$^\prime\alpha$)t} reaction, suggesting that
commercial reactors can operate efficiently with moderate enrichment.

Geometric parameter sweeps determined a saturation blanket thickness of 500--600\,mm, governed by
the neutron mean free path. Notably, water coolant was found to enhance breeding via spectral
moderation, whereas gaseous coolants increased leakage, presenting a clear trade-off between
neutronic efficiency and chemical safety.

Ultimately, while these results establish robust trends, the reliance on homogenised materials and
simplified sources likely overestimates TBR. Future work must therefore transition to high-fidelity
heterogeneous models—incorporating discrete cooling channels and realistic plasma sources—to provide
the conservative predictions necessary for the engineering design of next-generation fusion devices.

\section{Supplementary Neutronics Theory} \label{app:neutronics_theory}

\subsection{Elastic Scattering Kinematics}
In an elastic collision with a nucleus of mass number $A$, the average logarithmic energy decrement
per collision ($\xi$) is determined by the target mass \cite{nuclearphysics}:
\begin{equation}
    \xi = 1 + \frac{(A-1)^2}{2A} \ln \left( \frac{A-1}{A+1} \right)
\end{equation}
For Hydrogen ($A \approx 1$), $\xi = 1$, indicating that a neutron can lose all its energy in a
single head-on collision. For lead ($A \approx 208$), $\xi \approx 0.0096$, necessitating hundreds
of collisions to achieve the same thermalisation.
